1c:T228b,<h2>Research Question</h2>
<p><strong>Can latent prediction (JEPA) capture tonal structure better than discrete self-supervised models for African languages?</strong></p>
<p>Current SSL models (Wav2Vec 2.0, HuBERT, MMS) learn discrete units via k-means quantization of speech representations. Recent work shows these discrete units lose tonal information: pitch contours that carry lexical meaning in languages like Yoruba, Ewe, Kabiye, and Igbo are compressed away during quantization. This is a structural limitation, not a data limitation.</p>
<p>Audio-JEPA predicts latent representations directly (no quantization), which <em>could</em> preserve continuous pitch information. JALI tests this hypothesis rigorously on African tonal languages.</p>
<p>The name: <em>Jali</em> (also written Djeli) is the Mandinka word for griot, the traditional oral historian and storyteller in West African culture.</p>
<h2>Positioning: What Already Exists</h2>
<p>JALI does not claim to be the first African speech model. The landscape is already active:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>MMS</strong> (Meta, 2023): 1,406 languages in pre-training, 1,107 in ASR. Massive scale, but trained predominantly on religious text readings (narrow domain).</li>
<li><strong>AfriHuBERT</strong> (2024): 1,226 African languages/dialects, 10,000+ hours. Strongest African SSL baseline.</li>
<li><strong>Whisper</strong> (OpenAI, 2023): Supervised multilingual ASR, weak on low-resource African languages.</li>
<li><strong>FLEURS</strong> (Google, 2022): Benchmark covering 20+ African languages, but WER-only evaluation.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>What's missing</strong>: No existing model or benchmark specifically measures <em>tonal preservation</em> in SSL representations. WER conflates lexical, phonological, and tonal errors. The community lacks a tonal evaluation protocol.</p>
<h2>Three-Layer Research Program</h2>
<h3>Layer 1: Tonal Benchmark (the foundation)</h3>
<p>A standardized evaluation protocol for tonal African languages:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Languages</strong>: Yoruba, Ewe, Kabiye, Igbo (4 tonal systems: register, terraced, complex)</li>
<li><strong>Metrics</strong>: WER + CER + Frame Error Rate (FER) + <strong>Tonal Error Rate (TER)</strong></li>
<li><strong>TER definition</strong>: percentage of tone-bearing segments where the predicted tone (H/L/M/R/F) differs from reference, following recent work on tonal ASR evaluation</li>
<li><strong>Baselines</strong>: MMS, AfriHuBERT, HuBERT, Wav2Vec 2.0, Whisper, all evaluated under identical conditions</li>
<li><strong>Data splits</strong>: 1h / 5h / 10h labeled fine-tuning to test label efficiency</li>
</ul>
<p>This layer has standalone value regardless of JALI's architecture results.</p>
<h3>Layer 2: Representation Probing (the science)</h3>
<p>Systematic comparison of what SSL representations actually encode:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Models probed</strong>: Audio-JEPA vs HuBERT vs MMS vs AfriHuBERT</li>
<li><strong>Probing tasks</strong>:
<ul>
<li>Tone classification (H/L/M/R/F per syllable)</li>
<li>Pitch contour regression (F0 trajectory from frozen representations)</li>
<li>Phoneme discrimination (minimal tonal pairs: Yoruba <em>ọkọ</em> H-H vs <em>ọkọ</em> L-H)</li>
<li>Code-switching detection (language boundary in mixed utterances)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Analysis</strong>: Layer-wise probing to identify where tonal information lives (or dies) in each architecture</li>
</ul>
<p>This answers the central question empirically: <em>does latent prediction retain more tonal signal than discrete quantization?</em></p>
<h3>Layer 3: Architecture Innovation (the contribution)</h3>
<p>If probing confirms JEPA advantages, introduce targeted improvements:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tone-aware masking</strong>: Mask spectrogram patches aligned with pitch contours rather than random time segments. Forces the predictor to reconstruct tonal transitions, not just spectral content.</li>
<li><strong>Hierarchical encoding</strong>: Local phoneme/tonal encoder + global morpho-syllabic representation. Critical for agglutinative Bantu languages where morphological boundaries carry tonal patterns.</li>
<li><strong>Family-conditioned predictor</strong>: Condition the JEPA predictor on language family (Niger-Congo A, Niger-Congo B, Afroasiatic, Nilo-Saharan) to generalize across related languages.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-language masking</strong>: Mask spans that cross code-switching boundaries, forcing the model to learn language-invariant representations at switch points.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Data Strategy</h2>
<h3>Unlabeled (pre-training)</h3>
<p>No transcription needed. Sources:</p>
<ul>
<li>Online radio streams: BBC Hausa, RFI Afrique Yoruba/Swahili, national broadcasters (Togo, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya)</li>
<li>Podcasts in African languages (growing rapidly)</li>
<li>Existing corpora: CMU Wilderness, Bible.is audio (same domain as MMS, useful for controlled comparison)</li>
</ul>
<p>Target: 1,000-2,000 hours across 20+ languages. Curation (removing music, ads, silence) is the main bottleneck.</p>
<h3>Labeled (fine-tuning &#x26; evaluation)</h3>
<p>Small but clean. Quality matters more than quantity:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CommonVoice</strong>: Kinyarwanda (2,000h), Kabiye (50h+), Luganda, Yoruba</li>
<li><strong>FLEURS</strong>: 20+ African languages, ~10h each, read speech</li>
<li><strong>BABEL</strong>: Igbo, Hausa, Amharic (conversational speech)</li>
<li><strong>AfriSpeech</strong>: 200h accented English (useful for code-switching evaluation)</li>
<li><strong>Custom annotation</strong>: For TER evaluation, we need explicit tone annotation on a small subset (~2-5h per language). Can leverage linguistic fieldwork resources and Praat forced alignment with tone tier.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Feasibility</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Compute</strong>: Audio-JEPA pre-training on ~1,000h is feasible on 4-8 GPUs in ~1 week. Probing experiments are lightweight.</li>
<li><strong>Data</strong>: Radio audio is free and abundant. The bottleneck is tone annotation for evaluation (~2-5h per language, requires linguists).</li>
<li><strong>Risk mitigation</strong>: Even if JEPA shows no tonal advantage, Layers 1-2 (benchmark + probing study) are publishable standalone contributions.</li>
<li><strong>Community</strong>: Masakhane NLP, DSFSI (U. Pretoria), Lacuna Fund, active communities with African language expertise and datasets.</li>
</ul>
<h2>Why This Matters Either Way</h2>
<p>If JEPA preserves tone better → architectural contribution: latent prediction is the right paradigm for tonal languages. Directly actionable for ASR, language preservation, speech-based applications.</p>
<p>If JEPA does NOT preserve tone better → equally valuable: proves that the discrete vs. latent distinction is not the bottleneck, redirecting research toward other solutions (explicit pitch features, multi-task learning, etc.). The benchmark and probing methodology remain useful infrastructure.</p>
